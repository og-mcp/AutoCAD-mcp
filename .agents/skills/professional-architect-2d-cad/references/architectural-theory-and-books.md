# Canonical Architectural Books & Spatial Design Principles

This reference distills the core design principles taught in the foundational texts of architectural education and professional practice. Apply these rules whenever synthesizing, critiquing, or refining a building layout.

---

## 1. Francis D.K. Ching — *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*

### A. Primary Elements Defining Architectural Space
Every building is composed of two complementary systems: **Solid Mass (Poché)** and **Spatial Void (Room/Court)**.
1. **Horizontal Planes**:
   - **Base Plane**: The ground datum or floor slab that supports human activity.
   - **Elevated Base Plane (Plinth)**: Lifting a floor `0.30 m – 1.20 m` above surrounding terrain creates a sense of threshold, dignity, and separation from the street while protecting against ground moisture.
   - **Depressed Base Plane (Sunken Court/Conversation Pit)**: Lowering a floor plane creates an intimate, sheltered micro-space within a larger room without obstructing sightlines across the upper space.
   - **Overhead Plane (Ceiling / Roof Canopy)**: Controls acoustic scale and daylight. Varying ceiling heights (`2.40 m` compressed entry vestibule → `3.20 m+` released living pavilion) creates psychological **Compression and Release**.

2. **Vertical Planes**:
   - **Linear Elements (Columns)**: Define spatial zones and structural bays while maintaining visual and physical continuity between adjacent rooms.
   - **Single Vertical Plane**: Articulates a primary facade or divides a space into front and back without full enclosure.
   - **L-Shaped Plane**: Stabilizes a corner and creates a protected diagonal field of space outward from the inner corner.
   - **Parallel Vertical Planes**: Generate a strong directional axis and movement channel (corridor, gallery, colonnade).
   - **U-Shaped Plane**: Defines an inward focus with a single open orientation toward a view or courtyard.
   - **Four Planes (Closure)**: Complete tectonic enclosure; windows and doors must be carefully placed near corners (to wash adjacent walls with grazing light) or centered (to establish formal symmetry).

### B. Spatial Organization Typologies
Choose the spatial organization that fits the site and program:
- **Centralized Organization**: Dominant central space (courtyard, rotunda, great hall) with secondary rooms grouped around its perimeter.
- **Linear Organization**: Repetitive or varied spaces strung along a circulation spine or gallery facing a prime solar/view orientation.
- **Radial Organization**: Central core with linear wings extending outward into the landscape.
- **Clustered Organization**: Rooms grouped by functional proximity, acoustic zone, or shared structural/wet cores.
- **Grid Organization**: Spaces organized within a three-dimensional structural column-and-beam framework (`A–D` × `1–4`).

### C. Circulation: Movement Through Space
Treat circulation as the perceptual thread linking every plane of a building:
1. **Approach (Distant View)**: Frontal, oblique, or spiral path leading to the building entrance.
2. **Entrance (Threshold)**: Transition from exterior to interior—always articulate with a canopy overhang, recessed alcove, or change of floor material/elevation.
3. **Configuration of the Path**: Linear, radial, spiral, grid, or composite. Keep primary intersections clear and sunlit.
4. **Path-Space Relationships**:
   - **Pass by Spaces**: Integrity of each room is preserved; mediated by short threshold vestibules.
   - **Pass Through Spaces**: Path cuts axially or diagonally through a room, creating dynamic social zones (avoid in bedrooms/private studies).
   - **Terminate in a Space**: The location of the destination room establishes the axis; reinforce with a framed garden window or hearth.

### D. Six Ordering Principles
- **Axis**: A line established by two points in space, about which forms and spaces are arranged symmetrically or in balanced asymmetry.
- **Symmetry**: Bilateral or radial balance across a shared axis.
- **Hierarchy**: Articulating the importance of a primary space by exceptional size, unique geometry, or strategic placement.
- **Rhythm & Repetition**: Recurring structural bays (`e.g., 4.80 m × 4.80 m`), window mullions, or colonnade columns.
- **Datum**: A line, plane (such as a continuous plinth or flat roof canopy), or volume that gathers and organizes a varied pattern of spaces.
- **Transformation**: Adapting an architectural prototype (such as a courtyard house) to specific site constraints without losing its conceptual clarity.

## 2. Christopher Alexander — *A Pattern Language*

Apply these high-leverage architectural patterns when designing floor plans:

- **Pattern 106 — Positive Outdoor Space**: Never leave outdoor areas as leftover setbacks around a box. Shape building wings (L, U, or Courtyard configurations) so outdoor gardens read as purposeful "outdoor rooms" with partial perimeter enclosure.
- **Pattern 110 — Main Entrance**: Place the main entrance where it is immediately visible from the primary approach avenue, with a sheltered transition porch (`min. 1.80 m × 2.00 m`).
- **Pattern 127 — Intimacy Gradient**: Lay out rooms in a strict sequence from most public at the entry (`Foyer → Living / Dining / Courtyard`) to semi-private (`Kitchen / Study`) to most private (`Bedrooms / Baths`).
- **Pattern 130 — Entrance Room**: Provide a sunlit vestibule (`min. 1.80 m × 2.40 m`) with coat storage and a framed view toward the garden or social heart, while screening direct street sightlines into private areas.
- **Pattern 159 — Light on Two Sides of Every Room**: Whenever possible, locate habitable rooms at building corners or along courtyards so windows on two perpendicular or opposite walls eliminate harsh glare and create luminous, shadow-balanced interiors.
- **Pattern 179 — Alcoves**: Give large social rooms smaller lower-ceilinged edge zones (`reading nook, window bench, breakfast bay`) around `1.80 m – 2.40 m` deep.
- **Pattern 190 — Ceiling Height Variety**: Match ceiling height to room floor area—intimate spaces (`Bath, Study, Alcove: 2.40 m – 2.60 m`), standard rooms (`Bedrooms, Kitchen: 2.70 m – 2.85 m`), and major social spaces (`Living Pavilion: 3.00 m – 3.60 m`).

## 3. Matthew Frederick — *101 Things I Learned in Architecture School*

- **Figure-Ground Theory & Poché**: Read the white spaces (rooms and courts) and the black cut solids (walls, piers, service cores) with equal care. Use thick service cores (closets, wet walls, pantries) as **inhabited poché** to carve clean rectangular room proportions out of irregular footprints.
- **Aligning Geometry**: Align interior partition walls, kitchen islands, ceiling soffits, and exterior window mullions with the structural column grid and with each other. Every unaligned jog creates visual noise and construction errors.
- **Draw Not the Thing, But the Effect of the Thing**: In a 2D plan, use lineweight hierarchy (`0.50 mm` cut walls vs. `0.13 mm` furniture/tile) so the spatial volume leaps off the sheet before a single room label is read.
